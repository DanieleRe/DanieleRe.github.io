Focusing ultrasound through the skull

Low-intensity focused ultrasound can reach deep brain targets without surgery, but the skull bends and scrambles the beam. This is a live acoustic simulation of a 64-element array aiming at a deep, cingulate-like target through three different skulls. What the three planners do

Ignore the skull. Each element is delayed as if the head were water. The skull is about twice as fast as tissue and uneven in thickness, so the wavefronts arrive out of step: the beam splits, drifts off target, and stretches along its axis.

Ray planner. Delays are computed along straight lines from each element to the target, through the real skull's sound speed. It is fast enough for real-time planning, but it ignores refraction and the skull's internal texture, so it recovers only about two thirds of the achievable pressure.

Time reversal. Simulate a source at the target, record what reaches each element, and play it back time-reversed (phase-conjugated). It is the best a perfect skull model and a full wave simulation can do, and it is what the other two are measured against.

The finding: the planner, not the skull model, is the bottleneck. I also gave the ray planner a wrong skull, as a pseudo-CT from MRI would: ±10% bone sound speed, 1 mm extra thickness, or no internal texture. Each cost at most 0.03 of target pressure. The big loss is the straight-ray approximation itself (0.67 vs 1.00). That points to the most useful piece of AI here: a fast learned surrogate of the full-wave simulation (a neural operator) that closes the gap in milliseconds, with the skull-model accuracy then specified through that planner.

The focus is a cigar. Even when it lands, it is about 3 mm wide but 15–22 mm long. Which part of a deep structure was actually dosed is a question to answer for every patient, not assume.

Caveats: 2-D (no out-of-plane refraction), synthetic skulls rather than CT-derived ones, a simple second-order FDTD with crude bone loss and no shear waves. The live view runs on a coarser 0.5 mm grid than the 0.3 mm analysis, so its numbers differ slightly from the table; the ranking does not. Next step: CT-based skulls (e.g. the ITRUSST benchmark geometries) and a 3-D solver.
