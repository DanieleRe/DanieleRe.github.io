Timing pulses to a beat
a hands-on explainer

Can the timing of a small electrical pulse, rather than its strength, make it easier to see a phosphene, a flash of light produced by stimulating the eye? Play with the three ideas behind our study below.

Based on: Re, D., Ibrahim, R., & Hanein, Y. Pulses timed to temporal-interference envelope peaks increase phosphene detection with wearable dry-printed transorbital electrodes. Manuscript in preparation. Preliminary results: IEEE EMBC 2025.
Everything on this page is a teaching simulation with made-up parameters. It shows the ideas, not our data or results.

00The setup in 3D

Three pairs of electrodes, all dry-printed. A pulse pair above and below the right eye delivers brief biphasic pulses. A front pair beside the eye carries a 1000 Hz current, and a neck pair on the right side of the back of the neck carries 1005 Hz. Where the two carriers overlap they beat at 5 Hz, and the pulses are timed to that beat. Drag to rotate, and switch conditions at the top.

02Timing the pulse

Our question was not whether the envelope stimulates on its own. It was whether the same pulse becomes more effective when it lands on an envelope peak. Switch between the four conditions in the 3D view (00):

  • Pulses alone: the baseline.
  • On peaks: each pulse coincides with the envelope maximum.
  • On troughs: same carriers, pulse moved half a beat later, onto the minimum.
  • Unmodulated control: both carriers at the same frequency. At the pulse the kHz amplitude equals the peak condition, but there is no beat at all.

The clever comparison is the last one. "On peaks" and "unmodulated control" deliver the same kHz amplitude at the moment of the pulse. If peaks still win, it's the beat that matters, not just extra kHz current. In the 3D view's signal strip the band has the same height on peaks and with the unmodulated carrier; only the beat is missing.

One more subtlety from the head models: "peak" is defined by the two sources being in phase. Inside tissue that instant is an envelope maximum only where the two fields point the same way (Efront · Eneck > 0) and a minimum where they point in opposite directions. With the montage as wired, the "peak" pulses met the local maximum on 99.7% of the retina and all along the nerve to the apex; reversing the neck pair would have flipped that.

03Closing the loop

A wearable stimulator has to keep working for minutes or hours, but the threshold for seeing a phosphene isn't fixed: it drifts upward as the visual system adapts, and drops back after a rest. A fixed intensity that starts near threshold soon falls below it.

What the controllers do. Fixed never changes. Staircase steps down after two "seen" answers and up after one "not seen". Bayesian tracker keeps a probability distribution over where the threshold is, widens it a little every trial because the threshold drifts (like a Kalman filter), sharpens it with each answer, and picks the intensity that should be seen 70% of the time.

In the study we built this kind of simulation from the effects we measured, to choose a controller and to size the follow-up experiment. The design it points to is a two-level loop: a slow tracker for the drifting threshold, and fast timing of each pulse to the beat.
